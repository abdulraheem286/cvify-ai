To negotiate salary well, research a realistic range for the role before you talk numbers, let the employer name a figure first where you can, then make one clear, polite ask backed by the value you bring. That is the whole method. Everything else is timing and wording.

Most people lose money not because they are bad negotiators, but because they never ask at all. A short, calm conversation is often worth more than months of extra work, so it is worth preparing for properly.

## Research a realistic salary range first

You cannot negotiate a number you have never thought about. Before any call, find out what the role actually pays.

Look at job ads for the same title in the same city, since many now list a band. Check salary pages on job boards and professional communities. Ask two or three people in your field privately what a role like this pays. Pay varies a lot by country, city, company size, and industry, so filter for your situation rather than a global average.

Then write down three numbers:

- **Your walk-away number.** Below this, the job is not worth taking.
- **Your target.** A realistic, defensible number based on your research.
- **Your ask.** Slightly above your target, so there is room to meet in the middle.

Keep these on a note in front of you during every salary conversation.

## When should you talk about salary?

Timing changes your leverage. Early in a process you are one of many candidates. After an offer, you are the person they chose, and replacing you means starting over.

So the rule is simple: let salary come up when the employer raises it, and do your real negotiating after you have a written offer.

The one exception is the first screening call. Recruiters often ask about pay early to filter candidates. If your range is far outside their budget, it is better for everyone to find that out on day one. Give a researched range and move on.

## Your preparation checklist

Run through this before any conversation where money might come up:

- Research a pay range for your role, your level, and your city.
- Decide your walk-away number, your target, and your ask.
- List three to five concrete things you bring: skills, results, tools, certifications, languages.
- Note anything scarce about you, such as niche experience or a hard-to-hire skill.
- Decide which non-salary items you would happily trade for, like remote days or extra leave.
- Write your two or three key lines out and say them aloud once.
- Have the job description and your CV open during the call.

Saying the words aloud once matters more than it sounds. They feel awkward the first time and normal by the third.

## How to answer the salary expectations question

When you are asked what you are looking for early on, your first move is to hand the question back. You are not being difficult; you are asking for information they already have.

> "I'd like to understand the role a little better before putting a number on it. Do you have a budgeted range for this position? I'm happy to work from that."

Most recruiters will share a band. If they push and you have to answer, give a researched range rather than a single figure, and put your target near the bottom of it so the whole range stays acceptable to you.

> "Based on what I've seen for similar roles in this market, I'm looking somewhere in the range of X to Y, depending on the overall package. Does that fit what you had in mind?"

One thing to avoid: never answer with your current salary. If you are underpaid now, that number will follow you into the new job. If you are asked directly, it is fine to say you would rather focus on the value of this role.

## Anchoring: who names a number first

Whoever names a number first sets the anchor, and the rest of the conversation tends to orbit it. That is why letting the employer go first is usually better. Their band may be higher than you would have guessed.

If you have to anchor, anchor high but defensible. A number you can justify with research keeps the conversation friendly. A number pulled out of the air invites pushback.

Ranges are safer than single figures early on. Once you are countering a real offer, though, be specific. A single clear number is much easier to say yes to than a vague band.

## Justify with value, not need

This is the part people get wrong. Rent, bills, and family costs are real, but they are not reasons for a company to pay more. Value is.

Tie your ask to what you will do for them. Point at the job description, at the results on your CV, and at anything hard to replace about your skill set.

Here is a counter that does exactly that:

> "Thank you for the offer, I'm really pleased. Based on my research for this role and this market, I was hoping for closer to X. I've already run projects like the one in the brief and can pick up your tooling from day one, so would X be possible?"

Notice the shape: thank them, state a number, give a reason tied to their needs, ask a question. Then stop talking and let them answer. Silence is uncomfortable, but filling it usually costs you money.

## Negotiate benefits, not just base pay

Sometimes base salary really is fixed by a pay band or an internal structure. That is not the end of the negotiation, it is a redirection.

Things that are often more flexible than salary:

- Extra annual leave days
- Remote or hybrid working days
- A signing bonus, or an earlier salary review at six months
- A learning budget, courses, or a certification paid for
- Equipment, travel, or a home office allowance
- A better job title, which also helps your next role

> "I understand the base is fixed at that level, and I'd still love to join. Could we look at the wider package instead? Two extra leave days and a review at six months would make this an easy yes for me."

## Get it in writing

A verbal agreement is not an offer. Before you resign from anything, ask for the final package in writing: base pay, bonus terms, leave, start date, working pattern, and anything you negotiated.

> "That all sounds great, thank you. Could you send the final details over in writing so I can review and sign? I'll come back to you by Thursday."

Read it once, carefully. If something you agreed on is missing, mention it politely straight away rather than after you start.

## Know when to stop

Negotiation is one ask, sometimes two. After that, you are risking goodwill for small gains.

Stop when they say the number is final and you believe them, when you have won a real improvement and the rest is minor, or when the tone of the conversation changes. Accept warmly, even if you did not get everything. You will be working with these people on Monday, and how you close this conversation is the first impression they keep.

If the answer is a firm no and the offer still sits above your walk-away number, take it and put a note in your calendar for a review conversation in six months. Preparing for that one is usually easier than negotiating with a stranger.

## Key takeaways

- Research a realistic range before any conversation, and set a walk-away number, a target, and an ask.
- Let the employer name a number first where you can, and deflect the expectations question politely.
- Justify your ask with the value you bring, never with your personal costs.
- If base pay is fixed, negotiate leave, remote days, a learning budget, or an early review.
- Ask once, get the final package in writing, and close warmly whatever the answer.
