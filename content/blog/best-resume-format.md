For most people, the best resume format in 2026 is reverse-chronological: your most recent job first, then the one before it, and so on down the page. It is what recruiters expect to see, and it is the layout that hiring software reads most reliably. The other two options — functional and combination — still have their place, but only in specific situations.

This guide compares all three side by side, explains who each one suits, and covers the page setup details that quietly decide whether your resume looks professional or amateur.

## Why does resume format matter so much?

Format is not decoration. It decides the order a reader meets your information, and it decides whether software can pull that information out at all.

Most applications now pass through an ATS (applicant tracking system) — software that stores your resume and turns it into searchable text. Then a human skims what survived, often for well under a minute. A good resume format serves both: predictable structure for the software, and an obvious top-to-bottom reading path for the person.

Get it wrong and the strongest experience in the world can land in the wrong field of a database, or simply never get read.

## The three resume formats compared

| Format | Who it suits | Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Reverse-chronological** | Almost everyone: students, professionals, senior hires, anyone with a steady record in one field | Expected by recruiters; parses cleanly in an ATS; shows progression and recency at a glance | Highlights employment gaps and job hopping; makes a big career switch look less relevant |
| **Functional (skills-based)** | A last resort for dramatic career changes or very long breaks from work | Puts transferable skills first; downplays dates | Often distrusted by recruiters; hard for software to link skills to real roles; hides the context that makes skills believable |
| **Combination (hybrid)** | Career changers, freelancers, and specialists whose skills matter as much as their job titles | Skills up front plus a full dated history; flexible without looking evasive | Takes more space; needs tight editing to avoid repeating yourself |

## Why reverse-chronological wins for most people

Recruiters read hundreds of resumes and have learned one shape. When yours matches it, they can answer their two main questions immediately: what are you doing now, and does it relate to this job?

It also handles the boring practical things well. Dates sit next to job titles, so software can work out how long you did each thing. Achievements sit under the role they belong to, so context is never lost.

Use it if your recent work is broadly relevant to the job you want — even if your path has not been perfectly straight. A short gap or a sideways move is far less damaging than a layout that looks like it is dodging the question.

## Why are functional resumes risky?

A functional resume groups everything under skill headings — "Project Management," "Client Communication" — and leaves job history as a bare list at the bottom, sometimes with no dates at all.

Two problems follow:

- **Recruiters are suspicious of it.** The format is strongly associated with hiding gaps, short stints, or a thin work history. Even when you have nothing to hide, you inherit the suspicion.
- **Software struggles with it.** An ATS is built to connect a job title to an employer to a date range. When your achievements float free of any role, the parser may record skills with no evidence attached, and a recruiter filtering by years of experience in a job title may never see you.

If you are changing fields, the hybrid format below solves the same problem without the baggage. Our guide to a [career change resume](/blog/career-change-resume) walks through how to reframe experience you already have.

## When the combination format makes sense

A combination resume opens with a short professional summary and a focused skills section, then gives a complete reverse-chronological work history underneath.

That opening block does the persuading. The dated history does the proving. It suits people whose job titles undersell them: a career changer, a freelancer with many overlapping clients, or a technical specialist whose tools matter as much as their employers.

Keep the top section disciplined. Three or four lines of summary and a tight group of genuinely relevant skills is plenty. If the skills block pushes your recent experience onto page two, it is too long.

## How should you set up the page?

The format is the skeleton. These settings are the posture.

- **Margins:** roughly 0.5 to 1 inch on all sides. Below half an inch the page feels cramped and can clip when printed.
- **Body font size:** 10 to 12 point, with headings a couple of points larger. Use one clean font throughout — Calibri, Arial, Helvetica, Georgia, or similar.
- **Line spacing:** single, with a clear gap between sections. White space is what makes a page skimmable.
- **Alignment:** left-aligned body text. Justified text creates uneven word gaps that are harder to read.
- **Layout:** one column, top to bottom. Skip tables, text boxes, and sidebars — they are the most common cause of scrambled parsing.
- **Bullets:** simple round bullets, one to two lines each, three to five per role.
- **Consistency:** pick one date style, such as "Mar 2024 – Present," and use it everywhere.

## What order should the sections go in?

For a reverse-chronological resume, this order works nearly everywhere:

1. Name and contact details, in the body of the page — never only in the header or footer.
2. A short professional summary, two to four lines.
3. Work experience, most recent first.
4. Education.
5. Skills, plus certifications, projects, or languages if they are relevant.

Students and recent graduates are the main exception: put education above experience until you have a year or two of relevant work behind you. And keep the whole thing tight — see [how long should a CV be](/blog/how-long-should-a-cv-be) for the length that fits your stage.

## Which file type should you send?

Send a text-based PDF unless the job posting asks for something else. It keeps your layout intact on every device and stays selectable and searchable.

Check it in one second: open the PDF and try to highlight a word with your cursor. If you cannot, it is an image, and the software will read nothing at all.

Send a Word file only when the employer or recruitment agency specifically requests it. Either way, name the file plainly, such as Firstname-Lastname-Resume.pdf, so it is easy to find in a crowded inbox. ## Key takeaways

- Reverse-chronological is the right resume format for the large majority of applicants, and it is the safest default when you are unsure.
- Functional resumes hide the dated context that recruiters and hiring software both rely on, so use them only as a last resort.
- The combination format is the better answer for career changers: skills up front, full work history underneath.
- Page setup carries real weight — single column, standard font, sensible margins, consistent dates, real text.
- Save as a text-based PDF, and confirm you can select the text before you send it.
