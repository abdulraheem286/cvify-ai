To write an internship resume, lead with your education and relevant coursework, then back it up with projects, part-time or volunteer work, and skills that match the posting. You are not expected to have a career history yet, so the goal is to show that you can learn quickly and finish real work.

That changes what belongs on the page. Instead of years of job titles, you are assembling proof — a class project you shipped, a shift job you held down, a club you helped run.

## What order should the sections go in?

Employers spend seconds on a first pass, so the order does a lot of the work. For an internship application, use this sequence:

- **Contact details** — full name, phone, professional email, city, plus LinkedIn, GitHub, or a portfolio link if you have one.
- **Summary** — two or three sentences on what you are studying and what you want from the internship.
- **Education** — degree, institution, expected graduation date, and relevant coursework.
- **Projects** — coursework, capstones, hackathons, or anything you built on your own.
- **Experience** — part-time, seasonal, volunteer, or freelance work, most recent first.
- **Activities** — societies, clubs, sports, student government, community work.
- **Skills** — tools, software, and languages that appear in the posting.
- **Extras** — certifications, awards, languages, only when they are relevant.

If you have already done an internship or placement in the same field, move Experience above Projects. Otherwise leave the order as it is — your strongest material is your education and what you have built with it.

## How do you lead with education and coursework?

List your degree, institution, expected graduation date, and then a short line of relevant coursework. That coursework line is the part most students skip, and it is often the part that matches the posting most directly.

Pick four to six modules that relate to the internship rather than listing your whole transcript. If a module involved a substantial piece of work, promote it to your Projects section instead of burying it here.

> **BSc Computer Science — University of Manchester** | Expected June 2027
> Relevant coursework: Data Structures, Databases, Statistics, Human-Computer Interaction
> Society: Manchester Tech Club (events lead)

Add your GPA or grade only if it is strong or specifically requested. If it is average, spend that line on something you did instead.

## How do you write up projects when you have no job history?

Treat each project like a job entry. Give it a name, a date, one line on what it was, and one or two lines on what you actually did and what came out of it. Academic and personal projects both count, and so do group projects — just be clear about your own role.

The mistake to avoid is describing the assignment rather than your contribution. "Built a database for a class" tells a recruiter nothing. Say what you chose, what you built, and how it ended.

> **Campus Food Waste Dashboard** — University group project, Jan–Apr 2026
> Built a dashboard tracking unsold meals across three campus cafes for a data visualisation module.
> Wrote the Python scripts that cleaned and merged four months of till exports, and presented the findings to catering staff, who adopted the weekly report.

> **Budgeting App for Students** — Personal project, 2025
> Designed and built a simple React app for tracking a monthly student budget.
> Ran it past twelve coursemates, rewrote the categories based on their feedback, and published it with a short write-up on GitHub.

## Does part-time and volunteer work belong on an internship resume?

Yes. A retail shift, a summer warehouse job, tutoring, or a volunteer role all show that someone trusted you with responsibility and that you turned up. Recruiters read these as reliability, which is genuinely what worries them about hiring students.

Describe them the same way you would any job — action first, outcome second. Do not apologise for the role or label it "just" anything.

> **Barista — Cafe Nero, Lahore** | Jun 2025 – Present (part-time)
> Handle the morning rush alone twice a week, serving around 80 customers a shift.
> Trained two new starters on the till and drinks menu, and took over the weekly stock count after four months.

## Which transferable skills actually matter?

Transferable skills are abilities you built in one place that apply somewhere else — running a society taught you scheduling, a shift job taught you handling pressure. They matter for internships because you rarely have the exact technical experience yet.

The useful ones are specific and provable. Rather than listing "teamwork", show it inside a bullet: coordinating five people on a group project is teamwork with evidence attached. Keep your skills section for concrete, checkable things.

- **Tools and software** — Excel, Figma, Python, Google Analytics, SQL, whatever the posting names.
- **Technical methods** — data cleaning, user research, financial modelling, lab techniques.
- **Languages** — with an honest level, such as "Urdu (native), English (fluent)".
- **Certifications** — short courses that are genuinely relevant, with the year.

Leave out soft-skill filler like "hard-working" and "detail-oriented". Every applicant writes those, so they carry no information. For more on building a resume around a limited work history, see [how to write a CV with no experience](/blog/how-to-write-a-cv-with-no-experience).

## How do you tailor a resume to the internship posting?

Read the posting twice and mark the words it repeats — the tools, the responsibilities, the type of work. Then make sure those exact words appear on your resume wherever they are genuinely true of you.

This matters for two readers. A human recruiter is checking for an obvious match, and many companies run applications through an ATS (applicant tracking system), which is software that scans and filters resumes before a person sees them. Both respond to the posting's own vocabulary.

Tailoring is mostly reordering, not rewriting. Move the closest project to the top, expand the coursework that matches, swap "data analysis" for "data visualisation" if that is the phrase used, and cut a bullet that has nothing to do with this role. Ten minutes per application is enough.

## What do employers actually look for in interns?

They are not hiring a finished professional. They are asking three quiet questions: can this person learn fast, will they finish what they start, and will they be easy to work alongside for a few months?

Every section of your resume should answer at least one of those. Coursework and projects answer the first. Completed work with outcomes answers the second. Part-time jobs, societies, and volunteering answer the third.

Genuine interest shows too. An applicant whose projects, reading, or side work point in the direction of the role reads very differently from one sending the same document everywhere.

## Key takeaways

- **Lead with education and relevant coursework**, then projects, work, activities, and skills.
- **Write projects like jobs** — your role, what you did, and the outcome.
- **Keep part-time and volunteer work in**; it proves reliability, which is what worries employers about interns.
- **Tailor to the posting** by reusing its own vocabulary wherever it is honestly true of you.
- **Stay on one page** and export as a PDF with a sensible file name.
