To list certifications on a resume, write the full certification name, the organisation that issued it, and the date you earned it, plus the credential ID or expiry date when an employer may need to verify it. That single line, repeated in the same shape for every entry, is all the format you need. What trips most people up is not the format but the decisions around it: where the section goes, which certifications are worth the space, and what to do with ones that are expired or only half finished.

## What is the standard format for listing certifications?

Every entry follows the same pattern:

**Certification name — Issuing organisation, Month Year**

That is the core. Add a credential ID, expiry date, or location only when it adds something. A few real examples:

> **Project Management Professional (PMP)** — Project Management Institute, March 2025

> **Certified Public Accountant (CPA)** — State Board of Accountancy, June 2023 | Licence #123456

> **AWS Certified Solutions Architect – Associate** — Amazon Web Services, January 2026 | Expires January 2029

> **First Aid at Work** — Certified October 2025, valid through October 2028

Notice what stays consistent: the credential comes first, because that is the phrase a recruiter or a resume-scanning system is looking for. Spell out the full name and put the common abbreviation in brackets after it, so both versions are searchable. If you only write "PMP", a system searching for "Project Management Professional" may miss you.

Use the same date style everywhere on your resume. If your work history says "Mar 2025", your certifications should not say "March 2025".

## Where should you put certifications on a resume?

There are three sensible places, and the right one depends on how much the certification matters to the job.

- **A dedicated "Certifications" section.** This is the default for most people. Place it after work experience and education. It keeps everything in one scannable block.
- **Inside your education section.** Good if you only have one or two certifications and want to save space. List them under a sub-heading so they are not mistaken for a degree.
- **Next to your name or in your summary.** Reserve this for credentials that are the reason you qualify — think RN, CPA, PMP, or a legal practising licence. Adding it to your header line ("Sarah Ahmed, RN") means it is seen in the first two seconds.

One more option: if a certification is central to a specific job, mention it once in your professional summary and again in the certifications section. Repeating it there is not padding, it is making sure it lands.

## Which certifications are worth including?

The honest filter is relevance. Every line on your resume competes for attention, so a certification earns its place if it does at least one of these things:

- **It is required for the role.** Licences, safety tickets, and regulated credentials go on, always.
- **It proves a skill named in the job description.** If the ad asks for cloud experience and you hold a cloud certification, that is a direct match.
- **It is recognised in your industry.** Well-known credentials from professional bodies, software vendors, or accrediting institutions carry weight because employers already know what they involve.
- **It fills a gap.** Changing careers or short on experience? A relevant certification is evidence you have done the work to catch up.

Leave off certifications that are years out of date and unrelated, internal company badges nobody outside your old employer recognises, and anything so basic it raises questions rather than answering them. ## How do you list a certification you are still working on?

You can list an in-progress certification, as long as you say clearly that it is in progress. Never let it look finished.

Add the expected completion date and, if it helps, how far along you are:

> **Certified Information Systems Security Professional (CISSP)** — In progress, expected completion December 2026

If you have passed part of a multi-exam certification, say so: "2 of 4 exams passed". That is more useful than a vague "studying towards", and it is verifiable. Recruiters generally like in-progress credentials because they show current effort — but only if the date is realistic. If you have not booked the exam and have no timeline, it is probably too early to list it.

## Should you list expired certifications?

Sometimes, and only with the status shown. There are three cases:

1. **The credential still reflects a real skill.** List it with "Expired 2024" beside it. You knew the material, and you are being straight about the paperwork.
2. **You are renewing it.** Write "Renewal in progress, expected March 2026". That turns a weak point into a small positive.
3. **It is a licence for a regulated job.** Remove it. In fields like nursing, accounting, teaching, aviation, and the trades, an expired licence means you cannot legally do the work, and listing it without the status reads as misleading. Employers verify these, so there is nothing to gain from ambiguity.

For regulated roles generally, include the licence number and the state, province, or country that issued it. Licences are usually tied to a place, and a hiring manager needs to know yours covers where the job is.

## Do online course certificates belong on a resume?

They can, if you frame them accurately. A certification is usually awarded by a professional body or vendor after an exam or assessment. A course completion certificate says you finished a course. Both have value, but they are not the same thing, and mixing them into one list makes the whole section look padded.

The fix is a separate heading:

> **Courses and Training**
> Google Data Analytics Certificate — Google (via Coursera), 2025
> Advanced Excel for Business — Completed 2024

Group short courses together, keep them recent, and keep them relevant. Two or three that support the skills you are applying with beat a list of twelve. If you are not sure which skills to highlight alongside them, see our list of the [best skills to put on a CV](/blog/best-skills-for-cv-2026).

## Key takeaways

- Use one consistent format: certification name, issuing organisation, date, plus credential ID or expiry when it helps.
- Spell out the full name and add the abbreviation in brackets so both versions can be found.
- A dedicated certifications section below experience suits most people; header placement is for licences that qualify you for the job.
- List in-progress certifications with an expected date, and label expired ones honestly — or remove them entirely if they are licences for regulated work.
- Keep online course certificates under a separate "Courses and Training" heading so you are not overstating what you earned.

Certifications are one of the fastest ways to prove a skill without a long work history behind it, so once you have picked the right ones, give them a clean, consistent section and let them do their job.
