To prepare for a phone interview, find a quiet spot with strong signal, keep your CV and the job description in front of you, and have short notes ready on why you want the role, your notice period, and your salary expectations. That is genuinely most of the work. A phone screening rewards preparation more than charisma, because the person on the other end is ticking boxes, not judging your handshake.

The tricky part is that you lose everything you normally lean on: eye contact, a smile, a nod that says "keep going." All you have is your voice. Here is how to make it work for you.

## What is a phone screening actually for?

A phone screening (a short first call, usually with a recruiter rather than the hiring manager) exists to save everyone time. The company wants to confirm that you are real, interested, available, and roughly in budget before they book an hour of a manager's day.

That means the bar is different from a full interview. Nobody expects a brilliant answer about your greatest professional failure. They want to know: does this person match the CV, can they explain their experience clearly, do they actually want this job, and can we afford them?

Once you understand that, the call gets easier. Your goal is not to dazzle, it is to remove every reason to say no.

## Set yourself up before you pick up

Ten minutes of setup prevents most phone interview disasters. Get these in place before the scheduled time:

- **A quiet space.** No cafés, no shared kitchens, no rooms where someone might walk in. A parked car works surprisingly well.
- **Good signal or a landline.** Test the reception where you plan to sit. If your mobile is weak, use Wi-Fi calling or ask for a video call link instead.
- **Your CV, printed or open on screen.** The same version you sent them.
- **The job description.** Highlight the three or four duties that matter most.
- **One page of notes.** Bullet points only, in large text, so you can glance rather than read.
- **A glass of water.** Dry mouth is real, and a sip buys you a second to think.
- **Pen and paper.** For names, next steps, and anything you want to ask later.

Also silence laptop notifications and turn on Do Not Disturb, so no call-waiting beep cuts through the conversation.

## The questions worth rehearsing out loud

Screening calls are predictable. Practise these until they feel natural, ideally by saying them aloud rather than reading them in your head.

**"Walk me through your CV."** Keep it to about two minutes, move forward in time, and end on why you are looking now.

> "I started in customer support at a small software company, then moved into account management when they needed someone to handle renewals. I have spent the last three years looking after a portfolio of about forty business accounts, and last year I grew renewals in that portfolio by a fifth. I have enjoyed the relationship side most, which is why a client-facing role like this one caught my eye."

**"Why this role?"** Connect one thing about the company to one thing about you. Vague enthusiasm sounds like a mass application.

> "Two reasons. You are expanding into the education sector, and that is where most of my accounts have been, so I would not be starting from zero. And the job description mentions owning the full renewal cycle rather than just handovers, which is the part of the work I want more of."

**"When could you start, and what is your notice period?"** Be exact and honest, because recruiters plan around this.

> "I am on a one-month notice period, so realistically I could start four to five weeks after an offer. I have a little flexibility on the exact date if that helps."

## How do you sound confident without body language?

On the phone, your voice carries all the signals your face normally would. A few small habits change how you come across:

**Stand up or sit upright, and smile while you speak.** Your breathing changes, and so does your energy. Smiling sounds like a cliché, but it genuinely changes your tone, and the listener can hear it.

**Slow down.** Nerves speed everyone up. Deliberately talk slightly slower than feels natural, and pause at full stops.

**Let silences sit.** When you finish an answer, stop. Do not fill the gap by rambling and weakening a good answer. If the interviewer is typing notes, that pause is normal.

**Signal the shape of your answer.** Say "there were two main things," then actually give two. Without visual cues, this helps the listener follow you.

**Check in occasionally.** A quick "does that answer it, or would you like more detail?" replaces the nod you cannot see.

## Take notes, but stay in the conversation

Write down the interviewer's name at the start, plus anything about the role that was not in the advert: team size, reporting line, why the role is open, the interview stages. These details are gold for your next round.

Keep it to keywords, not sentences. Long note-taking creates dead air, and scribbling or typing can be audible, so press the keys gently or use paper.

## What if they ask about salary early?

Recruiters ask about money on screening calls because it is a filter, so being cagey rarely helps. Turn it around once: "I would rather match your budget than guess. What range is approved for this role?" Many recruiters will tell you, and then you can respond honestly.

If they push you first, give a researched range rather than a single number, and base it on the market rather than on your current pay.

> "Based on similar roles I have seen, I have been looking in the range of X to Y, depending on the overall package and the scope of the role. Does that fit what you had in mind?"

Two rules: do not quote a number so low that it caps your offer, and do not refuse to engage, because a screener with no range to record cannot move you forward.

## How do you close a phone interview?

The last two minutes matter more than people think. Do three things.

Ask one or two real questions. Something practical works best on a screening call, like "what does the interview process look like from here?" or "what would success in the first six months look like?"

Confirm the next steps. Ask what happens next and when you should expect to hear. It is completely normal to ask, and it saves you a week of refreshing your inbox.

Restate your interest in one line and thank them by name: "Thanks, Sarah. This sounds like exactly the role I have been looking for, so I would love to move to the next stage." Then send a short thank-you email the same day.

## Key takeaways

- A phone screening checks fit, interest, availability, and budget, so remove reasons to say no rather than trying to dazzle.
- Set up first: quiet room, good signal, CV, job description, notes, water, pen and paper.
- Rehearse the walk-through of your CV, your reason for applying, and your notice period out loud.
- Your voice does all the work, so smile, slow down, and let pauses sit.
- Handle salary with a researched range, and close by asking about the next steps.
