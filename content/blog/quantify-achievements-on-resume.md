To quantify an achievement, attach a number to its scale, time, money, or improvement: how many, how much, how often, how fast, or how much better than before. That single number is the difference between a line an employer skims past and a line they stop on. It turns a claim about you into evidence.

Most people write their resume bullets as a list of duties because that is how job descriptions are written. But the employer already knows what a sales assistant or a support agent does. What they cannot guess is how well you did it, and numbers are the shortest way to tell them.

## What does it mean to quantify an achievement?

Quantifying means answering the question a reader silently asks after every bullet: *how much?* If your bullet says you improved a process, the reader wonders by how much. If it says you managed a team, they wonder how big. Adding the figure closes that gap before it opens.

A quantified bullet usually has three parts: an action verb, what you did, and the number that proves the size or the result. For more on that first part, see our list of [resume action verbs](/blog/resume-action-verbs) that give a bullet a strong start.

## What kinds of numbers should you look for?

When you sit down with an old bullet point, run through these categories. One of them almost always fits:

- **Money** — revenue you brought in, budgets you controlled, costs you cut, orders you processed. "Handled a £40,000 monthly stock budget."
- **Percentages** — growth, reduction, accuracy, satisfaction, retention. "Cut returns by 18%."
- **Volume** — tickets, calls, customers, students, articles, deliveries, lines of code, invoices. "Processed 120 invoices a week."
- **Time saved** — how long something took before and after your change. "Reduced monthly reporting from two days to three hours."
- **People** — team size, people trained, clients managed, users supported. "Trained 12 new starters."
- **Frequency** — how often you did something. "Published 3 posts a week for 18 months."
- **Scale** — locations, countries, product lines, systems, square footage. "Supported 5 branch offices across two regions."

If none of these fit a bullet, that bullet may be describing a duty rather than an achievement. Rewrite the duty into an outcome first, then look again.

## Before and after: real bullet rewrites

Here is what the change looks like across different kinds of work. Notice how the "after" version is barely longer, but says far more.

> **Sales — Before:** "Responsible for selling products to new and existing customers."
> **After:** "Grew a territory of 60 accounts and hit 112% of a £250,000 annual target for two years running."

> **Customer support — Before:** "Answered customer queries by phone and email."
> **After:** "Resolved around 45 tickets a day and lifted first-contact resolution from 68% to 81% in six months."

> **Admin — Before:** "Managed diaries and organised meetings for the department."
> **After:** "Coordinated diaries for 9 managers and cut double-booked meetings to near zero by moving the team to a shared calendar system."

> **Teaching — Before:** "Taught GCSE mathematics and prepared students for exams."
> **After:** "Taught 4 GCSE maths classes of about 30 students and raised the department's grade 4+ pass rate from 71% to 84% over two years."

> **Developer — Before:** "Worked on improving the performance of the company website."
> **After:** "Reduced homepage load time from 4.2s to 1.1s by lazy-loading images and trimming the JavaScript bundle by roughly 40%."

> **Hospitality — Before:** "Helped run the restaurant floor during busy service."
> **After:** "Ran a 90-cover dinner service with a team of 6 and kept average table turnaround under 75 minutes."

Each "after" answers *how much* without adding a second line. That is the standard to aim for. If you want the surrounding structure right too, our guide on [how to list work experience](/blog/how-to-list-work-experience-on-cv) covers the format each of these bullets sits inside.

## How do you quantify when your role is not numbers-driven?

Plenty of jobs have no dashboard and no targets. Charity coordinators, carers, researchers, designers, and support staff often feel there is nothing to count. There nearly always is; it just was not being tracked for you.

Try these angles:

**Count the inputs, not just the outputs.** You may not know the impact of the newsletter, but you know you wrote 24 of them. You may not have a satisfaction score, but you know you supported 15 residents daily.

**Describe the scope.** The size of what you were trusted with is itself a number: a team of 8, a budget of $30,000, a caseload of 22 families, a catalogue of 400 products.

**Measure the before and after.** Think of one thing that was messy when you arrived and tidier when you left. How long did it take then? How long now? How many errors, complaints, or chasing emails did it produce?

**Use time and duration.** "Ran the volunteer rota single-handedly for 18 months" tells a reader about reliability, and it is a number.

**Count what you were the first or only person to do.** "Only Spanish speaker on a 14-person team, handling all Spanish-language enquiries" is quantified and distinctive.

If the honest answer is still that a bullet has no number, leave it alone. A clear, specific sentence beats a forced statistic every time.

## Being honest with estimates

Estimates are completely acceptable on a resume. Inflation is not. The line between them is whether you could explain the figure calmly if an interviewer asked where it came from.

A few rules that keep you safe:

- **Say "approximately", "around", or "over"** when the figure is not exact. It signals honesty rather than weakness.
- **Round down, never up.** If you handled 45 to 55 calls a day, write "around 45". You will never be caught short.
- **Show your working in your head.** If you served roughly 60 customers a shift over 200 shifts, "served over 10,000 customers" is defensible. If you cannot do that arithmetic, do not use the number.
- **Never claim a company result as your own.** "Contributed to a 20% revenue rise" is honest; "increased revenue 20%" is not, if a team of 30 did it with you.
- **Keep a note of where each number came from.** A short private list saves you in an interview months later.

Interviewers ask about numbers precisely because they are memorable. A figure you can back up becomes your best story. A figure you invented becomes the moment the interview turns.

## Key takeaways

- Quantify by attaching a number to scale, time, money, or improvement — answer the reader's silent "how much?"
- Run every bullet past the seven number types: money, percentage, volume, time saved, people, frequency, and scale.
- Rewrite duties into outcomes first, then add the figure; the "after" version should be barely longer than the "before".
- If your role is not numbers-driven, count inputs, scope, duration, or the before-and-after of something you fixed.
- Estimate honestly, round down, use "approximately", and never claim a whole team's result as your own.
