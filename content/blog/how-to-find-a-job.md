To find a job, get clear on the roles you want, fix your CV so it matches those roles, then apply steadily through several channels instead of relying on one. That order matters, because applying with a vague target and a weak CV just burns time and confidence.

A job search feels chaotic when you have no system. It feels manageable when you do. Below is a plain, repeatable plan you can start today, whether you are looking for your first role or your fifth.

## Start by deciding what job you are actually looking for

Before you open a single job board, write down two or three job titles you want and could realistically do now. Not a dream career ten years out. The next step.

To pick them, ask yourself three questions:

- What have I actually done before, in work, study, volunteering, or side projects?
- What parts of that did I do well and enjoy?
- What job titles appear on adverts that describe that kind of work?

That last one is important. Companies advertise using specific titles, and the same work can be called different things in different places. Read ten adverts that look interesting and note the titles, the skills that repeat, and the words they use. Those repeated words become the language of your CV and your applications.

Also decide your practical limits early: location, remote or office, salary range you need, hours, and industries you would rather avoid. Knowing this stops you from chasing roles you would turn down anyway.

## Why your CV comes before your applications

Your CV is the thing doing the work while you sleep. If it is out of date, generic, or hard to skim, every application you send is weaker than it could be.

Get the basics right first:

- A short summary at the top that names your target role and what you bring.
- Work experience written as achievements, not duties. What changed because you were there?
- Clear structure, plain formatting, real dates, no gimmicks.
- A skills section that matches the words used in the adverts you found.
- No typos. Read it out loud once, slowly.

## Where to look for a job

Most people use one channel and wonder why it is slow. Use several at once, because different roles surface in different places.

- **Job boards.** The big general boards plus any niche board for your field. Set up email alerts so new roles come to you.
- **Company career pages.** Make a list of companies you would like to work for and check their careers page directly. Some roles are posted there and nowhere else.
- **LinkedIn.** Keep your profile matching your CV, turn on the open-to-work setting if you are comfortable with it, and follow companies on your list so you see their posts.
- **Recruiters and agencies.** Find the ones that specialise in your field. A good recruiter is paid to place you and can tell you what employers are really asking for.
- **Referrals from people you know.** Former colleagues, classmates, tutors, friends of friends. Tell them what you are looking for in one clear sentence so they can spot it for you.
- **Speculative applications.** If a company you admire has nothing advertised, send a short email with your CV and one paragraph on what you could help with. It works more often than people expect, especially at smaller companies.

## A step-by-step plan to find a job

1. **Set your target.** Write down two or three job titles, plus your location, salary range, and any deal-breakers.
2. **Fix your CV.** Build one strong master CV that covers everything you have done. This is your base, not the version you send.
3. **Set up your sources.** Create alerts on two or three job boards, update your LinkedIn, and list ten companies to check directly.
4. **Tell people you are looking.** Message former colleagues and contacts individually. Short, specific, no pressure.
5. **Apply in batches.** Pick a set of roles, then tailor and send each one properly rather than mass-sending the same file.
6. **Log every application.** Company, role, date, link, contact name, status. A spreadsheet is fine.
7. **Follow up.** After a week or two with no reply, send one short, polite message.
8. **Review weekly.** Look at your log. Getting interviews but no offers is an interview problem. Getting no replies at all is usually a CV or targeting problem.
9. **Adjust and repeat.** Change one thing at a time so you can tell what actually made the difference.

## Should you tailor every application?

Yes, and it takes less time than people fear. You are not rewriting your CV from scratch. You are taking your master CV, reordering the top few bullet points so the most relevant experience comes first, and matching the wording of the advert where it honestly applies.

Read the advert twice, pick out the three or four things they clearly care about most, and make sure those are visible in the top half of your first page. That is most of the job done. ## How to keep track of your applications

A simple tracker turns a fog of applications into something you can manage. Use a spreadsheet with one row per application and columns for company, role, date applied, where you found it, contact name, follow-up date, and status.

This stops you applying twice to the same company, tells you when to follow up, and shows you which channel is actually producing interviews instead of leaving you to guess.

## What to do after you apply

Silence is normal and rarely personal. Hiring teams are slow, roles get put on hold, and internal candidates appear out of nowhere.

Give it a week or two, then send one short, polite message to the recruiter or hiring manager repeating your interest and asking about next steps. One follow-up is professional. Three is not. While you wait, keep applying. Never pause your search because one application feels promising.

## How do you stay motivated when you keep getting rejected?

Rejection is the normal texture of a job search, not a verdict on you. Most rejections are about fit, timing, or an internal candidate you never knew existed.

A few things that genuinely help:

- **Keep a routine.** Set fixed hours for searching, applying, and following up, then stop for the day. An endless search is exhausting and less effective.
- **Measure effort, not outcomes.** You control applications sent and people contacted. You do not control replies.
- **Batch the hard parts.** Do all your searching in one block and all your tailoring in another. Switching constantly is what drains you.
- **Keep learning something.** A short course, a small project, or a certificate gives you progress you can point to and something new to talk about.
- **Talk to people.** Isolation makes a slow search feel worse than it is.

A job search often takes weeks or months. Steady effort beats frantic bursts followed by weeks of nothing.

## Key takeaways

- Decide on two or three realistic target job titles before you apply to anything.
- Fix your CV first, then tailor a copy of it for every role you send.
- Use several channels at once: job boards, company sites, LinkedIn, recruiters, referrals, and speculative emails.
- Track every application in a simple spreadsheet and send one polite follow-up after a week or two.
- Keep a routine, expect rejection, and review your results weekly so you can adjust what is not working.
