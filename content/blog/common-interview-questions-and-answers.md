The most common interview questions are a small, predictable set: "Tell me about yourself", "Why do you want this job?", "What are your strengths and weaknesses?", "Why should we hire you?", "Tell me about a challenge", and "Where do you see yourself in five years?". Preparation is the real secret. If you plan a short answer for each of these before you walk in, you will feel calm and sound clear.

You do not need a script for every possible question. You need a few strong stories and a simple way to tell them. Below are the questions that come up again and again, with a quick tip and an example approach for each.

## Tell me about yourself

This is usually the opener. It is not an invitation to share your life story. Keep it to about 60 to 90 seconds and talk about your work.

Use three parts: what you do now, one or two results you are proud of, and why this role is your next step.

*Example approach:* "I am a customer support agent with three years of experience. Last year I helped cut our response time by a third by rewriting our help articles. I want to move into a support role with more training work, which is why this job caught my eye."

## Why do you want this job?

The interviewer wants to know you care about this role, not just any job. Show that you read about the company and the position.

Name one specific thing about the company or the work, then link it to your own goals.

*Example approach:* "I like that your team ships small updates every week instead of one big yearly release. I do my best work with fast feedback, so that pace fits how I like to work."

## What are your strengths?

Pick two or three strengths that match the job description. Do not just name them. Back each one with a quick proof point.

*Example approach:* "My main strength is organisation. In my last role I managed four projects at once without missing a deadline, using a simple weekly plan I built for the team."

## What is your biggest weakness?

This question tests honesty and self-awareness. Avoid fake weaknesses like "I work too hard". Pick a real but manageable one, then explain how you are improving it.

*Example approach:* "I used to hold onto tasks too long instead of asking for help. I now check in with my manager mid-project, which has made my work faster and less stressful."

## Why should we hire you?

Think of this as your short sales pitch. Match your top skills to what the role needs, and mention the value you would add.

*Example approach:* "You need someone who can handle both design and copy. I have done both for two years, so you would get one person covering two skills, and less back-and-forth between teams."

## Tell me about a challenge you faced

Here the interviewer wants a real story. This is where a simple structure saves you. Use the STAR method, explained just below.

Pick a challenge with a clear, positive ending. Keep it to your part in the story.

## The STAR method

STAR is a simple way to tell a work story without rambling. It stands for:

- **Situation** — the background. Set the scene in one or two sentences.
- **Task** — what you had to do, or the problem you had to solve.
- **Action** — the steps *you* took. This is the main part. Use "I", not "we".
- **Result** — what happened. Add a number if you can, like time saved or money earned.

*Example approach:* "Our online orders kept failing at checkout (Situation). I was asked to find the cause fast (Task). I tested the checkout step by step and found a broken payment link, then worked with a developer to fix it (Action). Sales recovered within a day and complaints dropped (Result)."

Use STAR for any question that starts with "Tell me about a time" or "Give me an example of".

## Where do you see yourself in five years?

The interviewer wants to know you are thinking ahead, and that this job fits your path. You do not need an exact plan.

Show ambition that lines up with the role, without saying you plan to leave soon.

*Example approach:* "In five years I would like to be leading small projects and mentoring newer staff. This role is a strong step toward that because of the responsibility it offers early on."

## Why are you leaving your current job?

Keep this positive. Never bad-mouth your old boss or company, even if it was a hard time. Focus on what you are moving *toward*.

*Example approach:* "I have learned a lot in my current role, but I have grown past what it can offer. I am looking for a place where I can take on bigger challenges, which is what drew me here."

## Do you have any questions for us?

Almost every interview ends with this, and "No" is the wrong answer. Asking questions shows you are serious and helps you judge if the job is right for you.

Prepare two or three questions in advance, such as:

- "What does success look like in the first 90 days?"
- "What are the biggest challenges the team is facing right now?"
- "How would you describe the team's working style?"
- "What are the next steps in the hiring process?"

Avoid asking only about salary or holidays in the first interview. Save those for later stages.

## A few habits that help

Strong answers matter, but so does how you show up. A few simple habits make a big difference:

- Research the company and re-read the job post the night before.
- Practise your answers out loud, not just in your head.
- Bring short stories with real results, not vague claims.
- Slow down. A short pause to think looks confident, not nervous.

## Key takeaways

- The common interview questions are predictable, so preparation is what sets you apart.
- Keep "Tell me about yourself" short: what you do now, a proud result, and why this role.
- Use the STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for any "tell me about a time" question.
- Stay positive when explaining weaknesses or why you are leaving.
- Always ask two or three thoughtful questions at the end.
