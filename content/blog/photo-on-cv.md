Whether you should put a photo on your CV depends almost entirely on where you are applying: usually no in the US, UK, Canada, Australia, and Ireland, but common and sometimes expected in parts of Europe, Asia, Latin America, and the Middle East. There is no global standard, which is why the advice you find online often contradicts itself.

The safest approach is to treat the photo as a local custom, not a personal choice. Below is how the norms break down, why so many employers now prefer CVs without photos, and how to take a good one if you do need it.

## CV Photo Norms by Region

Hiring culture varies a lot from country to country. This table gives you a general sense of what is normal, not a legal rule.

| Region | Photo expected? | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| US, Canada | Almost never | Leave it off unless the employer asks |
| UK, Ireland | Almost never | Leave it off; it can read as unprofessional |
| Australia, New Zealand | Rare | Leave it off unless the posting requests one |
| Germany, Austria, Switzerland | Often expected | Include a well-taken, professional headshot |
| France, Spain, Portugal, Italy | Common | A photo is normal and generally accepted |
| Eastern Europe | Common | Follow the job posting and local templates |
| Middle East | Common | Often expected, especially in the Gulf region |
| Much of Asia | Varies widely | Normal in some markets, unusual in others |
| Latin America | Common | A photo is widely used and rarely a problem |

If the company is international, the norm that matters is the one where the hiring team sits, not where the company was founded. Applying from Lahore to a London role means you follow UK practice.

When you are unsure, look at three things: the job posting, the application form, and the careers page. If any of them mention a photo, follow that. If none of them do, and you are applying in an English-speaking market, leave it out.

## Why Do So Many Employers Prefer No Photo?

The main reason is bias. A photo reveals age, gender, ethnicity, and other personal characteristics that have nothing to do with whether you can do the job. These details can influence decisions even when nobody intends them to.

Because of that, many organisations have moved towards more structured or anonymised screening. Some strip out names and photos before a hiring manager sees anything. Others simply ask candidates not to send photos at all.

There is a practical side too. In markets with strong equal-opportunity hiring practices, it is easier for a recruiter to avoid photos entirely than to prove they ignored one.

None of this means an employer will think badly of you for adding a photo. It means the photo creates work and risk for them, and gives you nothing in return. ## How Does a Photo Affect ATS Parsing?

Most medium and large employers use an ATS (applicant tracking system), which is software that reads your CV and turns it into structured data such as name, job titles, dates, and skills.

Images are not text, so an ATS cannot read anything inside a photo. That part is harmless. The problem is what a photo does to the layout around it.

Photos are usually placed inside a text box, a table cell, or a page header, and those are exactly the elements that parsing engines handle badly. The result can be:

- Your name or contact details being skipped, because they sit beside the image rather than above the content.
- Text read out of order, so your job titles and dates get mixed together.
- A larger file that some upload forms reject.
- Blank space where your summary should be, if the parser dropped the whole container.

Modern systems handle this better than they used to, but you have no way of knowing which system a given employer runs. If you want the details on what does parse cleanly, see our guide to [an ATS-friendly CV format](/blog/ats-cv-format).

A simple rule: if the application goes through an online portal rather than straight to a person's inbox, favour a clean, single-column, text-only CV.

## When Is a Photo Worth Including?

There are cases where a photo helps, or is simply required:

- **The employer asks for one.** Always follow the instruction in the posting.
- **You are applying where it is the norm.** In Germany, for example, a missing photo can make an application look incomplete.
- **Your appearance is part of the job.** Acting, modelling, presenting, and some hospitality and front-of-house roles use photos as a working requirement.
- **You are sending a personal portfolio or networking profile.** These are not screened like CVs, so the rules are looser.

Outside those cases, the photo is decoration. Space on a CV is limited, and a headshot takes up room that a strong summary or an extra achievement bullet would use better.

## How to Take a CV Photo That Helps You

If you do include one, a weak photo is worse than none at all. Use this checklist:

- **Recent and recognisable.** It should look like you today, not five years ago.
- **Head and shoulders.** Your face should fill most of the frame, cropped around chest height.
- **Plain background.** A blank wall or a softly blurred one. No bedrooms, no crowds, no holiday scenery.
- **Soft, even light.** Face a window in daylight. Avoid overhead lights that cast shadows under your eyes.
- **Dress for the industry.** Wear what you would wear to an interview at that company.
- **Relaxed, natural expression.** A slight smile reads as approachable. A stiff or overly formal face does not.
- **A decent camera, held properly.** A modern phone in good light beats a bad studio photo. Use the rear camera on a stand, not a selfie at arm's length.
- **No filters, sunglasses, or group crops.** Never cut yourself out of a social photo.
- **Small and correctly placed.** Keep it modest in size and put it in the header near your name.
- **Saved cleanly.** Use a standard JPG or PNG, sized so it stays sharp in print without bloating the file.

Take several shots, then pick the one where you look most like the person who would walk into the interview.

## What If You Cannot Tell What the Employer Wants?

Default to no photo. A CV without one is accepted everywhere, including the countries where photos are common. A CV with one is rejected in some places and quietly penalised in others.

You can also keep two versions of the same CV: a plain, text-only one for portals and English-speaking markets, and one with a headshot for markets that expect it. The content stays identical, so it is a two-minute job.

## Key takeaways

- Whether to put a photo on your CV depends on the country, so follow local norms rather than one global rule.
- In the US, UK, Canada, Australia, and Ireland, leave the photo off unless it is specifically requested.
- Photos are common in much of continental Europe, the Middle East, Latin America, and parts of Asia.
- Many employers avoid photos to reduce bias in hiring, and some will not accept CVs that include one.
- Photos sit inside layout elements that can confuse an ATS, risking scrambled or missing details.
- When in doubt, leave it out, and keep a separate photo version only for markets that expect it.
